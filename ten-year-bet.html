Alphaist: A New Fund’s Ten-Year Bet

Alphaist Partners was founded at the end of 2024. This interview describes the firm as it began investing in 2025 and reflects its founder's views at the time.

After leaving 5Y Capital, investor Peter made a major decision: to launch Alphaist Partners, an early-stage, dollar-denominated venture fund.

The timing seemed improbable. Over the preceding two years, the venture market had entered what many considered its deepest and most punishing downturn in years.

Fundraising had all but frozen. In the first eleven months of 2024, the number of new funds established in China's venture capital and private equity market fell 46% year on year. Geopolitics and other restrictions had made matters worse: by some estimates, almost 95% of dollar-fund LPs were no longer willing to invest in China.

While Peter was raising capital overseas, one LP told him that his fund ticked almost every box on their list of things to avoid: China, early-stage investing, and a first-time fund.

Peter also lacked three things. He had never run a fund as a GP, had yet to realize an exit from his investments, and had no team beyond one finance colleague. He was, in every sense, a solo VC.

Yet there is always that remaining 5%. Sometimes it makes all the difference.

Peter ultimately raised tens of millions of dollars from entrepreneurs, investors, and overseas institutions. At the time of the interview, Alphaist Partners had completed its first investment and was working on new deals.

He was untroubled by the pessimism that had swept through the industry the previous year. He was convinced that venture capital would endure, and that this vintage would be the best of the decade before and the decade ahead.

His confidence came from a belief that a new innovation cycle had begun. Just as startups needed new ways of building, investing needed new people and approaches to break old habits. He was also starting with little to lose, which gave him the freedom to be fearless.

Before launching Alphaist, Peter spent seven years investing in AI and robotics at 5Y Capital, backing companies including HAI Robotics, AgileX Robotics, Lightelligence, and Horizon Robotics.

Earlier in his career, after studying electrical engineering, he worked on smartphone communications at BlackBerry and on Google Glass at Google, specializing in embedded systems and signal processing.

In July 2023, Peter and his 5Y colleague Kaiyan He were excited to read Waves' interview with DeepSeek founder Wenfeng Liang. Having studied DJI founder Frank Wang and the entrepreneurs who had come out of DJI, Peter said the interview "immediately set off a familiar radar." We began our conversation with a question that had recently dominated discussions in the industry: why had Chinese venture capital missed Wenfeng Liang?

What follows is Peter's account, edited for publication.

Why Chinese VCs were almost bound to miss Wenfeng Liang

When a market is full of investors chasing buzzwords, inflating bubbles, and crowding into "star teams," it is hardly surprising that they underestimate, or fail to understand, a founder who is deeply committed to an ideal, even to the point of obsession.

Investors shaped by the internet era look at daily active users, retention, and revenue. They are used to driving growth, subsidizing users, and even assembling teams and deals to make a business happen. That approach rests on two features of internet businesses. First, marginal costs are close to zero: adding another user costs almost nothing. Second, network effects can turn a platform into a winner-take-all business once it has enough users.

Another inherited assumption is that large AI models have the same data flywheels as search and recommendation engines. Investors assume that more users and more usage will make the model smarter. Model startups have spent enormous amounts on promotion in pursuit of daily active users and retention. Some investors have even dismissed open-source strategies because they see no business model.

DeepSeek has challenged those assumptions. When a model reaches a truly high level of intelligence, feedback from ordinary users is of no use. While model intelligence still has so much room to improve, users will quickly move to more capable models. And making advanced models open source has created the world's fastest-growing and most valuable developer ecosystem.

Like DJI, DeepSeek represents a different kind of company from those of the internet era. The competition is no longer simply about business models and user numbers. These companies represent a new way of building technology businesses.

When technology makes a breakthrough or a step change, an internet VC's established ideas about business models and defensibility are of little use. What matters is whether a company is focused and efficient enough to be the first to cross a critical technical threshold. Money cannot solve the problems at this stage. It can even be harmful, as investors pressure companies to chase superficial metrics.

I have a particular radar for founders like Wenfeng Liang. When we first met in 2024, I remarked on how much he reminded me of Frank Wang. I spent seven or eight years as an engineer at BlackBerry and Google, and I have come to know many people from DJI. They share an uncompromising engineering mindset and a singular focus on the work.

Liang's views on talent and R&D are strikingly similar to DJI's: hire and develop fresh graduates, and pursue original work rather than imitation.

I once backed a founder who had come out of DJI. His team was developing a new product, and I happened to know an excellent hardware product manager who had worked on something similar. I offered to introduce them so he could learn from that experience. He turned me down immediately: "Why would I listen to how someone else did it instead of figuring it out for myself from scratch?"

That is why the founders I most admire, who are uncompromising, deeply focused, and think from first principles, are often better suited to this kind of company-building. They filter out 99% of the noise, avoid the shortcut of copying others, and concentrate on innovation around the critical problem. That is very close to how I think.

Many of the great technology companies, including Apple, Cisco, NVIDIA, Qualcomm, Huawei, and DJI, raised very little capital or did not need venture money.

Some peers say VCs missed DeepSeek because it was not raising money. Others say that when you find a great company, you should get on your knees if that is what it takes to invest. I do not think either is the right mindset. If you have to beg your way in, it means the company sees no value in you beyond a check.

The question I would rather ask is how I can better serve founders who are underestimated or misunderstood. I instinctively sympathize with idealists, and I understand what it takes to build a technology company. But I also want to help founders solve difficult problems and support them at the moments they truly need it, like DJI in 2010, or High-Flyer, Liang's quantitative investment firm, before 2015.

Why launch a new dollar fund now?

My confidence comes from two changes: how technology companies are being built, and the market we are in.

Building a technology company is very different from building an internet business. Internet startups typically combine low complexity with high uncertainty. The business model is simple, experimentation is fast, and once the model is validated, spending heavily on growth can be enough to win.

Two kinds of investors did well in that era. One was the philosopher, the deep thinker who could reason through the business from the top down: what creates network effects, or how a marketplace works. Uber and Didi, for example, are two-sided networks. Ofo and Mobike are time-based rental businesses. The former can become monopolies; the latter cannot.

The other kind excelled at winning access to deals or following the leading companies. Because markets were winner-take-all, momentum investing worked well in internet businesses.

Those rules do not hold in an era of technological innovation. Analogy, induction, and deduction, the tools internet-era generalist VCs know best, cannot fully explain an early DJI, Unitree, or DeepSeek.

Technology companies have both high complexity and high uncertainty. Their accumulated advantages cannot be reduced to a single business model. They may advance through several distinct leaps. You cannot understand them entirely from the top down, or win users and markets overnight simply by spending money.

Investors now need good judgment about technology. First, they need a clear sense of both its direction and its pace. My engineering career offers two examples. BlackBerry overestimated users' attachment to physical keyboards and underestimated how quickly 3G and app stores would spread. Google Glass overestimated the pace of progress in optics and displays, and underestimated how firmly the smartphone had established itself as people's primary device.

Second, do not oversimplify technology or mythologize it.

One of the most common mistakes I see among robotics investors is failing to distinguish a demo from a product. They underestimate engineering complexity and the demands of reliability. At the same time, technology offers no single secret weapon and no insurmountable barrier. The real moats often have the least to do with technical sophistication: network effects, scale, and brand.

How do you develop good judgment about technology? Stay close to the work, understand how the technology actually functions, and listen to engineers.

How did I know DeepSeek was exceptional? When its first model, DeepSeek Coder, came out in late 2023, my 5Y colleague Kaiyan He heard from founders building AI coding tools just how good it was. On coding benchmarks, it outperformed Code Llama, then the leading open-source coding model, and even GPT-3.5 Turbo.

The current market is also one that truly rewards the best founders and VCs.

In hot markets such as 2020 and 2021, many large venture firms were making a hundred investments a year. How do you find your way through that much noise? When there is no market beta, or beta is negative, a strategy of covering the market broadly is bound to lose money. That is precisely the environment in which investing for alpha makes the most sense.

I believe 5Y's approach of choosing a small number of companies and investing heavily in them is a fundamental truth of venture capital. People know that 5Y led the angel rounds in Xiaomi, Kuaishou, and Horizon Robotics. Fewer realize how much capital it continued to invest in their later rounds.

I take the same approach. I have made relatively few investments over the years, but in more than 80% of them I was the lead investor and held a board seat. With good companies, I will lead two or three rounds in succession. At AgileX, I led two consecutive rounds and invested in three rounds in total. Even after the third, its robotic lawn mower was not yet in mass production and had generated no revenue. A year later, it became one of the leading companies in the industry.

The overseas institutional LPs backing me share that conviction in an alpha-driven strategy. They know their peers are talking about "ABC," or "Anything But China." They know many GPs will struggle to raise subsequent funds, and competition for high-quality assets will ease. Those are precisely the reasons they are willing to back a first-time fund investing in China today.

I have noticed some common traits among these LPs. They have made substantial returns before. Almost all have invested in both 5Y Capital and Capital Today, firms with distinctly alpha-driven approaches and strong long-term track records. They know this strategy can work.

I am grateful for what I learned at 5Y. Beyond teaching me to concentrate capital in a few carefully chosen companies, Qin Liu told me when I first entered the industry that venture capital exists to "encourage entrepreneurship and create long-term value." When I later felt uncertain about investing, he told me that the essence of venture capital was to "find your Lei Jun," the founder of Xiaomi.

That is why Alphaist's values are a commitment to truth, passion for the work, and a long-term perspective.

Seeking truth means returning to first principles and the facts, relying less on analogy, induction, and deduction. That is not easy in private markets. The temptation is to chase the leading companies everyone agrees on: their valuations rise over a few rounds, and it can take years for a thesis to be proven wrong.

There is also an agency problem in venture capital. The boss is far from the actual work and relies entirely on junior colleagues for information.

Two recent conversations capture what I mean by a long-term perspective. I told Kathy Xu, the founder of Capital Today, that DJI was a great company, but that it was a shame it would not go public because investors could not exit. She immediately asked, "Why would you want to exit a great company?" Another time, an LP asked about my fund's term. I said mine was a twelve-year fund. His response was: "Twelve years is too short."

Many people do not understand why I would choose this moment to start a firm.

The answer I gave LPs while fundraising was simple: over the next ten years, China will produce ten companies like DJI. I want to partner with four or five of them at the earliest stages.

Why has a robotics investor backed no humanoid companies?

The answer is simple: I believe humanoid and general-purpose robots are still in their infancy. The hardware, software, and applications need a long time to develop. Market hype does not change the difficulty of the task or the time it will take.

The reason for building humanoid robots is that they are designed to operate in environments made for people. But humans are highly evolved organisms. The dexterity, dynamic capabilities, and power density of our fingers, bodies, and muscles far exceed what today's batteries, motors, and gear reducers can deliver.

We either need entirely new approaches to actuation and energy, or we need to tailor applications very precisely to what current technology can do. Otherwise, today's humanoid hardware will struggle to meet the demands of human environments.

On the software side, the algorithms humanoid robots need are far more complex than those for autonomous driving. From the 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge, it took autonomous driving twenty years to begin reaching real-world deployment. Given the demands of reliability and rare, long-tail situations, humanoid software could well follow a similar path.

Finding applications is an even greater challenge. Humanoids will compete with all kinds of purpose-built robots. Investors often compare them to smartphones replacing feature phones, but the analogy does not hold. The first smartphones were primarily a software revolution; hardware architecture and cost changed relatively little. Users could enjoy the benefits without paying much of a premium.

When Tesla announced its entry into China in 2014, it delivered more than 30,000 Model S cars worldwide that year.[1] Tesla had already solved the scientific and engineering problems and built a business at scale. A hundred new-energy vehicle companies quickly appeared in China, yet the market did not take off fully until 2020 and 2021. Have humanoid robots reached an equivalent point?

An investor recently asked me: "Haven't all the humanoid and embodied AI investments already been made? What is left for you to invest in if you focus on robotics?" It reminded me of a friend at another fund in early 2024 who said: "We invested in the angel round of one of China's 'six AI tigers,' backed a hot AI application, and invested in AI infrastructure. We have finished building out our AI portfolio. What is left for you?"

If everyone agrees on an opportunity from the outset, either I have understood it too late, or it may not be suited to startups in the first place.

The best startup opportunities begin in places most people doubt, where there are few competitors and large companies see little reason to pay attention. That is what makes the upside so large if you succeed. When I met Unitree founder Xingxing Wang in 2019, he was tinkering with AlienGo, his quadruped robot. He had already spent three years pursuing a direction almost nobody cared about. No investor would have imagined that Unitree, selling a few dozen robot dogs a year to enthusiasts and research institutions, would one day become one of the world's leading humanoid robotics companies.

There is also a saying in venture capital that companies valued above $100 million in their first financing round do not become great successes. In my experience, that holds in both China and the United States.

Every investor knows the Gartner Hype Cycle. The question is whether you can invest in the earliest wave. If you run a small early-stage fund, it is perfectly reasonable to back a number of sought-after teams at the angel stage, exit at Series B, and make ten times your money in a few years. But if your goal is to help young founders achieve something great over the long term, the choice is clear: join a game of passing the asset to the next buyer, or focus on work you believe in that others still doubt.

Robotics is nevertheless a major focus for Alphaist. It has the qualities I look for in an investment: a wave that will last more than ten years, an uncertain path, small initial markets, and little incentive for large companies to commit substantial resources. That is where early-stage venture capital has an opportunity.

Finding founders who build from nothing and insist on the impossible

I am not looking for founders whose advantage is privileged access to resources, or who simply want to repeat a previous success. I want to find those who create something from nothing and insist on doing what others believe cannot be done.

I once invested in an early-stage robotics company whose founder looked like an amateur by almost any measure. He had no engineering background, no experience developing products or taking them into mass production, and could not even assemble a credible team. What moved me was his willingness to repeatedly tear down and rebuild his own assumptions, his all-consuming commitment to the work, and a vision far ahead of its time.

If you study the best companies, you find that their success was rarely the inevitable result of the resources and conditions they started with. An extraordinary entrepreneur made something possible when everyone else believed it was impossible.

Making GPUs was not a particularly glamorous business when NVIDIA started. Could Intel not have done it? Of course it could. But CPUs were a bigger business, and Intel did not think GPUs were worth its attention. Jensen Huang was driven by a vision of general-purpose computing. He wanted to go beyond graphics rendering, build a software stack, and develop CUDA. More than a decade of work came before the AI boom.

In 2015, Horizon Robotics was among the earliest companies in the market building AI chips. Its founder, Kai Yu, was an algorithms researcher who had never designed a chip and had no automotive industry experience. He, too, was driven by a vision far ahead of its time and the determination to pursue it. Even when the company's direction was still unsettled, he insisted on tackling one of the hardest problems: chips for autonomous driving. Many people, including investors, asked why such a strong algorithms team would not build a software-only business, such as facial recognition. Kai insisted that algorithms alone would leave the company without a moat.

The best founders keep doing what is right when their teams waver, investors question them, and the market sends negative signals. That takes enormous inner resolve.

One of DJI's core values is a phrase that calls for intense passion, an uncompromising pursuit of excellence, and the determination to give everything to a long-term mission. That is my picture of an ideal founder.

Alphaist wants to be among these founders' earliest partners, to stay with them the longest, and to make the greatest difference to their companies.

Professor Zexiang Li is a remarkable example. When few people believed in Frank Wang and his team was falling apart, Li stood by him, provided investment that kept the company alive, and introduced students who could join the team. Without that support, the company might not have survived.

Elon Musk also has one VC friend he trusts above all others: Antonio Gracias of Valor Equity Partners. In 2007, when severe Roadster cost overruns brought Tesla close to bankruptcy, Gracias took on much of the work of reducing supply-chain costs. During the Model 3's "production hell" in 2018, he stayed at the factory with Musk, sleeping only a few hours a night and working directly on manufacturing problems.

2025 will be a very good time to start. The widespread adoption of AI, exemplified by DeepSeek, introduces an enormous new variable. I look forward to seeing it give rise to new companies, just as iOS once did.

Robotics, smart hardware, and intelligent driving are all moving rapidly into deployment and growing in volume.

When we look back from 2035, my bet is that AI and robots will be everywhere, and a new generation of founders will have built more companies like DJI and DeepSeek.

We will be glad we were part of one of the best vintages.
